# EPISODE 7 — "Twenty-Two Days"

SFX: A courtyard at midday. A microphone being tapped twice. Two hundred people, standing, because there are not enough steps.

JORAWAR: Louder? Can the back hear? Good.

SFX: Feedback, killed.

JORAWAR: Three things and then you can all get out of the sun. One. The circuit will be re-cut. Two. It will be sealed on the full moon of Vaisakh, twenty-two days from this morning. Three — and this is the only one that matters, so the men at the back should stop talking — nothing goes into the new circuit that is not on paper and registered.

SFX: Two hundred people making one sound.

MAN AT BACK: Registered where?

JORAWAR: Jhilan. Sub-registrar. Same as land, because it is land. A turn of worship is immovable property, gentlemen — that is not my opinion, that is the Supreme Court, and Phoolchand vakil will tell any of you the same for fifty rupees. If your day is in a book, that is a nice thing for the book. If it is not registered, it is a story your father told you.

NARRATOR: Every word of that was true. That is what made it work.

SFX: Cut. Close. The edge of the crowd.

TEJMAL: (low, urgent) He heard. Somebody in that room went straight to him, and I will find out which one, and —

KESAR: It doesn't matter which one.

TEJMAL: It matters to me.

KESAR: Sahib, look at what he just said. He didn't deny the book. He didn't even mention my grandmother. He moved the whole thing onto paper because he knows I don't have any.

TEJMAL: (a pause; he is not stupid, only aggrieved) ...Do you have any?

KESAR: I have a broom.

SFX: Nothing.

TEJMAL: Then in twenty-two days you have a broom and he has two hundred and fourteen days.

SFX: Cut. The storeroom. Late. One lamp.

GIRDHARI: I read it out. I have read it out and it has cost me the roll and my afternoon tea and forty years of quiet, and now you want more.

KESAR: I want to know what "registered" means.

GIRDHARI: It means Jhilan. It means a government book, not mine. It means two witnesses and a stamp and a fee, and it means a man called Hukum who has said no to every person who has ever walked in there because saying no is free and saying yes has consequences.

KESAR: Then I go to Jhilan and I register the book entry.

GIRDHARI: (almost gently) Child. You cannot register a page out of my book. My book is a private register of a village shrine. It is evidence of what happened. It is not the thing itself.

KESAR: Then what is the thing itself?

GIRDHARI: The deed.

SFX: Nothing.

KESAR: What deed.

GIRDHARI: In sixty-eight somebody wrote a document. My entry says "the said nine days having been sold". A writer does not put "the said" in front of something unless he is copying it off a paper in front of him. There was a paper. Somebody drafted it, somebody put her thumb on it, and my grandfather's successor copied its words into this column.

KESAR: Where is it?

GIRDHARI: Where every paper in this taluka is.

SFX: Cut. Morning. A bus. The specific hell of a Rajasthan district bus — horn, gears, forty people, a radio.

SITARA: (over the noise) Ji, sit. Sit properly, you're on the edge. So. You want the sixty-eight deed.

KESAR: You have a car.

SITARA: I have a car in Jhilan and a driver who is at his sister's wedding and I am on a bus with you, which tells you how badly I want the same thing. Understand?

KESAR: Why do you want it?

SITARA: (a flat laugh) Because I have paid money for days in this circuit and I am beginning to think I have bought weather. Never mind. Listen. Phoolchand vakil. Second floor above the tyre shop. Eighty-one years old. He has drafted every document in this taluka since nineteen fifty-nine — every sale, every mortgage, every will, and every single paper Jorawar ji's house holds.

KESAR: Then he'll never give it to me.

SITARA: No. He won't.

SFX: The bus hitting a pothole. Everyone lifting off the seat and coming down.

SITARA: But here is the thing about old vakils, ji, and I want you to hear it because you are going to need it: he did not draft it for Jorawar. Jorawar was five years old in sixty-eight. He drafted it for Jorawar's father, and Jorawar's father is dead, and a man of eighty-one is not frightened of a dead man's son. He is frightened of one thing only.

KESAR: What?

SITARA: Being asked to read out something he wrote when he was twenty-four.

SFX: Cut. A stairwell. Concrete, echoing, the smell-sound of a tyre shop below — a compressor, hissing, over and over.

SFX: A door with a nameplate that rattles.

PHOOLCHAND: (very old, very slow, and he repeats the last thing said to him — this is how he thinks) Closed. It's closed.

KESAR: Vakil sahib. Barkheri.

PHOOLCHAND: Barkheri. ...Barkheri. Whose?

KESAR: Mohari's.

SFX: Nothing. A compressor hissing downstairs. A long, long silence in an old man's office.

PHOOLCHAND: Mohari.

KESAR: Nine days. Sixty-eight.

PHOOLCHAND: (not to her) Sixty-eight.

SFX: A chair. Old wood. A man not getting up.

PHOOLCHAND: Come back Thursday.

KESAR: Sahib, there are twenty-two days —

PHOOLCHAND: Twenty-two days. Yes. Yes. I heard the announcement, everybody has heard the announcement, the tyre shop has heard the announcement. Come back Thursday.

KESAR: Will it be here Thursday?

PHOOLCHAND: ...

KESAR: Sahib. Will it be here Thursday?

SFX: An almirah. A big steel almirah, unlocked with a key from around a neck, opened four inches, and shut again.

PHOOLCHAND: Girl. Do you know who drew the deed you are asking me for?

KESAR: You.

PHOOLCHAND: Me. Yes. Me, at twenty-four, in this room, on that table, in a hand I was proud of.

SFX: Nothing.

PHOOLCHAND: And do you know who drew the paper Jorawar's grandfather held before it, and the paper his father held after it, and the block, and the mortgage on the tank land, and the settlement of sixty-two, and every single sheet of paper that man's house has ever stood on?

KESAR: (getting it, one second too late) ...You.

PHOOLCHAND: Twenty-two days. Come back Thursday. And bring somebody with a car, because whatever is in that almirah, girl, it was written by the same hand on both sides.
