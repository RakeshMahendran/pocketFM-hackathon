# EPISODE 10 — "The Fourteenth of Magh"

SFX: A padlock. Then coins. A donation box turned over onto a steel tray, and it goes on, and on, and on.

RUKMANI: (counting, out loud, because that is how it is done and because eighty people are watching) Ten. Twenty. Fifty. ...Fifty and five.

SFX: Notes now. The soft, serious sound of counted paper.

RUKMANI: Two hundred. Five hundred. One thousand.

SFX: Eighty people breathing.

RUKMANI: Two thousand two hundred. Two thousand nine hundred. Three thousand one hundred and forty, and eleven rupees fifty in coin. Three thousand one hundred and fifty-one rupees fifty paise, twenty-ninth of Chaitra.

SFX: Nothing.

GIRDHARI: (quietly) That's the biggest ordinary morning since the tank was dug.

RUKMANI: I know. I write it every morning. It's the biggest ordinary morning in eleven years.

NARRATOR: Two hundred people had come at four in the morning to hear a sweeper ring a bell. Every one of them put something in the box on the way out. That is not devotion and it is not politics. It is simply what happens when a village finds out it has been watching something for fifty-seven years without looking at it.

SFX: A steel tray pushed across a table.

RUKMANI: Sahib. Whose?

SFX: Nothing. Long.

JORAWAR: You wrote it down.

RUKMANI: I did.

JORAWAR: Then you know whose.

SFX: A man standing up and leaving a room, and eighty people getting out of his way.

RUKMANI: Kesar. Come and take it. No — count it first, in front of me, and then take it, because in nine days somebody is going to say you took more.

SFX: A girl counting money. Badly. Starting again.

KESAR: (very quietly) Three thousand one hundred and fifty-one.

RUKMANI: And fifty paise.

KESAR: And fifty paise.

SFX: Nothing. Long.

NARRATOR: Fifteen years. Ninety-one lamps a night, five thousand and some mornings on that rope. Forty rupees a day from a stranger, and now this. She stood in the shrine office with three thousand one hundred and fifty-one rupees and fifty paise in her two hands, and the first thing she felt was not joy. Say it plainly: she was ashamed. She had been given, in one morning, more than her mother had been given in thirty-one years.

SFX: Cut. A lane. Fast.

KESAR: Ma. Ma —

SFX: A sagwan door being shut before she reaches it.

KESAR: Ma, open it. Ma. Two hundred people came. Tejmal sahib said her name — he said Mohari's nine days, out loud, in front of the whole village, and Rukmani wrote it in the book —

SFX: Nothing.

KESAR: Ma. Why were you standing at the back.

SFX: A bolt drawn. A door opened four inches. A woman who has not slept.

BHANWARI: How much was the box.

KESAR: What?

BHANWARI: How much.

KESAR: Three thousand one hundred and fifty-one.

BHANWARI: (a sound, not a word) Ha.

KESAR: Ma —

BHANWARI: Bring it inside. Don't stand in a lane with money in your hands, everybody can see you, bring it in.

SFX: A door. A one-room house. A lamp lit for once.

SFX: Money set down on a floor.

BHANWARI: Forgive me.

KESAR: No. Not tonight. Tonight you talk.

BHANWARI: Forgive me. Eat first.

KESAR: MA.

SFX: Nothing. Very long. A lamp guttering.

BHANWARI: (finally, and the apologising voice is gone, and what is underneath it is much older) Sit down. Not there. Sit where you sleep. That's where she died. Sit there.

SFX: A girl sitting on a floor.

BHANWARI: Your grandmother told everybody the days were lent for two seasons. Narmada will have told you that.

KESAR: She did.

BHANWARI: She told it to me too. Every day for eleven years, and then she stopped talking, and then she died, and I was twenty-eight and there was nobody left in this house who could stand in front of that courtyard. Do you know what I did?

KESAR: You swept it.

BHANWARI: I swept it. For nineteen years. And you think that's shame. It isn't shame. Shame is what came before it.

SFX: Nothing.

KESAR: The fourteenth of Magh.

BHANWARI: The fourteenth of Magh.

SFX: A tin box being pulled out from under a bed. Old tin. It sticks.

BHANWARI: I can't read. You know I can't read. So I have kept a paper in this box for nineteen years without knowing what most of it says, and I have known what one line of it says because a man read that one line to me while I put my thumb on it.

SFX: Paper. Government paper, folded small, soft with handling.

KESAR: Give it.

BHANWARI: Wait. Wait. Let me say it first, because if you read it first you will not let me say it.

SFX: Nothing.

BHANWARI: When your father died you were four months in me. Four months. And a widow with a dead husband and a mother-in-law who has lost her days and a child coming is not a person in this village, she is a problem, and problems get solved by whoever has a jeep.

KESAR: Jorawar.

BHANWARI: He came to this door with a clerk and a stamp pad. Same as sixty-eight. Same lane, same tin, same pad. And he was kind. He has always been kind. He said — he said, "Bhanwari, the girl needs a birth entry or she is nobody, no school, no ration, no marriage, nothing. I'll get it done in Jhilan on Monday."

SFX: Nothing.

KESAR: And?

BHANWARI: And I put my thumb on it and I said thank you, sahib, and I have said thank you every day for nineteen years.

KESAR: Ma. What is wrong with the paper.

BHANWARI: (and now, at last, plainly) There is a column on it for the father.

SFX: Nothing. Long.

KESAR: My father is dead. He died before I was born. Everybody in Barkheri —

BHANWARI: Everybody in Barkheri knows it, yes. Barkheri is not a government building. Read the column.

SFX: A folded government form, opened.

NARRATOR: Nineteen years. She had swept that courtyard and eaten that kitchen's food and rung that bell, and every single one of those days there had been a piece of paper in a tin box under her mother's bed with somebody else's name on it. Not a lie anybody had told her. A lie nobody had ever needed to tell her.

KESAR: (reading, and her voice goes flat, which is what her voice does) "Name of child — Kesar. Village — Barkheri. Date — fourteenth Magh. Mother — Bhanwari, widow."

BHANWARI: Go on.

KESAR: "Father —"

SFX: Nothing. Very long.

BHANWARI: Say it out loud. Nineteen years I've had it under a bed. Say it out loud in this room where she died.

KESAR: The father's column doesn't say my father's name. It says Tejmal.
