# EPISODE 4 — "The Book Doesn't Have Her"

SFX: A car door. The town car pulling away down the track, gone. Then only wind, and a broom lying where it was dropped.

KESAR: Forty rupees.

SFX: Coins. Actual coins, counted into a palm, and then counted again because it is the second time she has ever done it.

NARRATOR: She did not buy anything with it. She walked to the storeroom, where the ledger lives, and she spent it on a question.

SFX: A low door. Damp. Mice. The particular smell-sound of a room full of paper — pages settling, a fly.

GIRDHARI: Shut it behind you. The damp gets in.

KESAR: Girdhari kaka.

GIRDHARI: I don't have anything for you.

KESAR: I haven't asked.

GIRDHARI: You've been standing outside that door since Tuesday. I have eyes and the door has a gap.

SFX: A chair. An old man sitting down the way old men sit down, in stages.

KESAR: Twenty-ninth of Chaitra to the seventh of Vaisakh.

GIRDHARI: (nothing)

KESAR: Nine days.

GIRDHARI: The book says what the book says.

KESAR: Then let the book say it.

SFX: Silence. Then, reluctantly, a heavy cloth-bound register being pulled across a table. The sound is enormous in a small room.

GIRDHARI: Sit. Not on the sacks, on the floor. Good.

SFX: Pages. Thick, brittle, turned slowly.

GIRDHARI: See this hand? This is Ratanlal. He wrote this book from nineteen thirty-one to nineteen sixty. Look how he makes a five. Nobody makes a five like that any more. Now look here — nineteen sixty-one, the hand changes. That's his son. Look how tired the son's hand is. You can read a man's whole life off a column of dates, if you know how.

KESAR: Kaka. Twenty-ninth of Chaitra.

GIRDHARI: (a long breath) Yes. Yes. All right.

SFX: Pages, further back. Stopping.

GIRDHARI: Nineteen sixty-seven. Twenty-ninth Chaitra to seventh Vaisakh. Nine days. And there is a name here.

KESAR: Read it.

GIRDHARI: I'm reading it.

KESAR: Read it out loud, kaka.

GIRDHARI: Mohari. Widow. Nine days, held whole, not by turn, since her husband's death.

SFX: Nothing. Long.

KESAR: Say the rest of it.

GIRDHARI: There's no rest of it. Sixty-eight, the column changes. Different hand, different ink, and the nine days are inside manager sahib's father's block from then to now. Fifty-seven years. That's all the book says.

KESAR: Who was she to me?

GIRDHARI: (very quietly) Child. Go home.

KESAR: Who was she to me?

GIRDHARI: Your father's mother. And she died in the room you sleep in, and your mother has spent nineteen years making sure you never heard her name, and now you have heard it from me, and I am too old for what happens next.

SFX: The register closing. Hard.

KESAR: What happens next?

GIRDHARI: The book doesn't have that page.

NARRATOR: He was fond of her. He had given her food out of the shrine kitchen every winter of her life and never once told anyone he was doing it. Remember that, because of what he did with his afternoon.

SFX: Cut. Outside. Afternoon heat, a bicycle bell, gravel. An old man walking somewhere he has decided to walk.

SFX: The big iron gate. Oiled. It does not creak.

JORAWAR: Girdhari. In this heat. Sit. Not the step — take the chair, you're seventy.

GIRDHARI: Sahib.

JORAWAR: What a good stick that is. Shisham?

GIRDHARI: Neem, sahib.

JORAWAR: It'll outlast us both. Say it.

SFX: Nothing. A fly.

GIRDHARI: The girl came to the storeroom.

JORAWAR: They do.

GIRDHARI: She had a date. She had the exact date, sahib. Twenty-ninth Chaitra.

SFX: A teacup being set down on a saucer with no sound at all, which takes care.

JORAWAR: Where did a sweeper get a date?

GIRDHARI: There was a woman here. From Jhilan. A car.

JORAWAR: Ah.

GIRDHARI: Sahib, I said nothing that isn't in the book. The book is the book. I only read what —

JORAWAR: Girdhari. Am I angry?

GIRDHARI: No, sahib.

JORAWAR: I'm not angry. You've kept that book forty years and you'll keep it forty more, and when the circuit is re-cut next month somebody has to read the new roll out to the village, and I can't think of another voice they'd believe. Can you?

SFX: A long, awful pause. An old man understanding exactly what he has just been paid.

GIRDHARI: No, sahib.

JORAWAR: Good. Go home the shady way.

NARRATOR: He walked home the shady way. He did not eat that night. Neither of those facts helped anybody.

SFX: Cut. Evening. The courtyard, full — the weekly committee sitting, thirty or forty villagers on the stone, the second bell being rung badly somewhere behind.

RUKMANI: (reading) Donation box, week ending: four thousand two hundred and ten. Oil: nine hundred. Board is short one name and has been short one name for eleven days and I am writing that down again.

JORAWAR: Write it down. Kesar.

SFX: Every conversation in a courtyard stopping at once.

KESAR: Sahib.

JORAWAR: Come to the front. No — the front. Where they can see you.

SFX: Bare feet on stone. Forty people breathing.

JORAWAR: You've been asking questions in the storeroom.

KESAR: I asked one question.

JORAWAR: In front of everybody, then. Ask it here. Go on. Ask me your one question and I'll answer it in front of the whole committee and we can all go and eat.

SFX: Nothing. Long.

NARRATOR: She was frightened. She counted, because that is what she does when she is frightened — forty people, two lamps out, one boy on the wall who should have been in bed.

KESAR: (small, steady) There are nine days in the circuit with no name on them.

JORAWAR: (immediately, gently, before she has finished the sentence) Nine.

SFX: Nothing.

JORAWAR: That's the number, isn't it. Nine. Twenty-ninth of Chaitra to the seventh of Vaisakh. Nine days. I've been waiting eleven years for somebody in your house to come and say that number to me out loud.

KESAR: I haven't said it yet.

JORAWAR: No. I said it. Sit down, everybody, we're not finished — Girdhari, bring the book, we'll do this properly, in the open, where nobody can say afterwards that anything was hidden.

SFX: Forty people not moving.

JORAWAR: Nine days, Kesar. And you came to a courtyard full of witnesses to ask me a question you didn't know the answer to.
