# EPISODE 13 — "Forty Days of Mud"

SFX: A pen set down on a government desk. Forty people in a doorway making a noise they will all deny later.

JORAWAR: Are you finished?

HUKUM: I'm finished, sahib.

JORAWAR: What a good pen that is.

SFX: Heavy shoes going down thirty-one steps, unhurried, all the way, in silence.

NARRATOR: He did not shout in the corridor. He did not shout in the jeep. He drove nineteen kilometres back to Barkheri and he stopped once, at the tea stall on the Dausa road, and he made one telephone call, and the man he telephoned was his brother.

SFX: Cut. Barkheri. Night. Rain, properly now — on tin, on mud, in gutters. The monsoon a fortnight early.

SFX: A door. Not the sagwan door. A heavier one.

TEJMAL: (already angry) Forty days. Forty days of mud, and it's started in Chaitra this year, in Chaitra, so it'll be fifty.

JORAWAR: Sit down, brother.

TEJMAL: I'm not sitting in your —

JORAWAR: Sit down. There's fish. Rukmani made fish.

SFX: A chair. Rain.

JORAWAR: Do you remember what our father gave you?

TEJMAL: Sixty-one days.

JORAWAR: Sixty-one days. And do you remember how he gave them to you? Not in a block. Scattered. Six here, four there, eleven in Shravan when the road is a river. He did that on purpose, and you have known it was on purpose since you were twenty-two, and you have never once said it to my face.

TEJMAL: (a beat) ...You knew that.

JORAWAR: I've known it thirty years. I've also known you'd stand next to a sweeper girl at four in the morning if it made me look at you. And it worked. Look. I'm looking.

SFX: Rain.

JORAWAR: Sixty-one days. Continuous. Twenty-second of Chaitra to the twenty-first of Vaisakh. Both fair days in the middle.

SFX: A chair not moving. Nothing. Long.

TEJMAL: Both fair days.

JORAWAR: Both.

TEJMAL: You'd give me both fair days.

JORAWAR: I'd give you the whole spring, brother, and I'd take the mud. I'm sixty-two. I've had the spring for thirty years and I've watched you sit in the water and I'd rather do this now than have you at my funeral not crying.

NARRATOR: State it plainly: he meant about a third of that. The third he meant is what made the rest of it work.

SFX: A steel plate. Fish.

TEJMAL: And.

JORAWAR: And an affidavit.

SFX: Nothing.

TEJMAL: Saying what.

JORAWAR: Saying what is true. That Mohari gave the days up before any paper was drawn. That she said it in her own room, in front of the family, the way everything in this village has always been done. That the vakil's sheet was written afterwards to tidy it, badly, by a boy of twenty-four who put the wrong word in it. Which is exactly what happened, Tejmal. You know it's what happened. It is what happened in every house in this taluka in nineteen sixty-eight.

TEJMAL: I was eleven years old in sixty-eight.

JORAWAR: You were eleven. You were in the house.

SFX: Rain on tin.

TEJMAL: I don't remember it.

JORAWAR: Nobody remembers anything. Everybody signs.

SFX: A chair pushed back.

TEJMAL: No.

JORAWAR: Sit down.

TEJMAL: I said no. Give the days to whoever you like, I've had the mud thirty years, I can have it thirty more —

JORAWAR: Sit down, Tejmal, there's a second thing.

SFX: Nothing. Rain.

SFX: A tin box. Government paper, folded small, soft with handling.

TEJMAL: What's this.

JORAWAR: A birth entry. Fourteenth of Magh, nineteen years ago. Registered at Jhilan on the Monday.

TEJMAL: Whose?

JORAWAR: Read the mother's column first. Then read the other one.

SFX: Paper. A man reading. A very long time. Rain.

TEJMAL: (and his voice is not the same voice) ...This is my name.

JORAWAR: It is.

TEJMAL: This is MY NAME. On a — this is —

JORAWAR: Sit down.

TEJMAL: I never touched that woman. I never — Bhanwari was my brother's tenant, she swept the courtyard, I have never in my life —

JORAWAR: I know.

SFX: Nothing.

TEJMAL: (barely) You know.

JORAWAR: I put it there. Nineteen years ago, with a clerk and a stamp pad, in that lane. A widow with a dead husband and a child coming needed a father's column filled and I filled it, and I filled it with your name because if I had used a stranger's name somebody would have asked who, and nobody ever asks who about a brother.

SFX: Rain.

JORAWAR: It cost me forty rupees and it took out the only door between that child and Mohari's nine days, and I have not thought about it once in nineteen years until Tuesday.

TEJMAL: You made me — the whole village has watched me stand next to that girl —

JORAWAR: Yes. Twice. In front of two hundred people, and then in front of a committee, and Rukmani wrote both down.

SFX: Nothing. Very long.

JORAWAR: So on Thursday morning, one of two things gets read out in that courtyard. Either your affidavit, and you take the spring. Or that paper, and you take the mud, and you take it with the whole taluka believing you fathered a sweeper's child and spent nineteen years letting her wash your brother's floor.

SFX: Rain. Fish going cold on a steel plate.

TEJMAL: It's a lie.

JORAWAR: It's registered. That's the whole of the law, brother, and it took me thirty years to learn it.

SFX: Nothing. Long. Then a chair.

TEJMAL: Where do I sign.

SFX: Cut. Wednesday. Midday. The storeroom. Rain on the roof, dripping through, into a bucket, every four seconds, all the way through this scene.

GIRDHARI: Sit down, child.

KESAR: I've got the book four extract. Serial two hundred and six. Hukum sahib gave me a certified copy, it cost eighty rupees, it's got the seal on the back —

GIRDHARI: Sit down.

SFX: Nothing. Drip.

KESAR: What.

SFX: Paper. Town paper, stiff, typed, with a stamp on it.

GIRDHARI: This came to the committee at nine. Rukmani has entered it. I have to read it out tomorrow morning in front of the village, because that is what I promised and because it is my job and because I am seventy years old and I have already spent everything I had.

KESAR: Whose is it?

GIRDHARI: Read it.

SFX: A page.

KESAR: (reading, flat) "Affidavit. I, Tejmal, son of Bhairon Singh, aged fifty-eight, resident of Barkheri, do solemnly affirm —"

SFX: Drip.

KESAR: (still flat) "— that in the month of Vaisakh, in the year nineteen sixty-eight, in the residence of Mohari, widow, in my presence and in the presence of the family, the said Mohari did orally and of her own will surrender the said nine days to my father, and that the deed thereafter drawn by Phoolchand vakil was drawn merely to record the same and contains an error of the writer."

SFX: Nothing. Very long. Drip.

GIRDHARI: Child.

KESAR: He stood next to me at the rope.

GIRDHARI: I know.

KESAR: Two hundred people. He said her name.

GIRDHARI: I know. Child, listen to me now, because tomorrow is the full moon and I can only be brave once more and I would like to spend it well. Read it again. Not the words. The month.

SFX: A page. Rain in a bucket.

KESAR: "In the month of Vaisakh, in the year nineteen sixty-eight."

GIRDHARI: And now say me the date on the deed in your bag. The one Phoolchand read to you off his own hand.

SFX: Brittle paper unfolding.

KESAR: The eleventh of January.

SFX: Nothing.

KESAR: He has sworn he watched her give the days up in April, and the deed that sold them was written and thumb-marked three months before he says she opened her mouth.
