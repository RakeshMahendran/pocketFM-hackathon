# EPISODE 9 — "The Twenty-Ninth of Chaitra"

SFX: One sheet of brittle paper, held. The compressor starting up downstairs, suddenly, loud.

KESAR: Sahib, I don't understand what you're telling me. It says sold. You wrote sold. Sold is sold.

PHOOLCHAND: Sold is sold. Yes. Everybody says that. Kanhaiya says it, Jorawar says it, the tyre shop says it. Sit down.

SFX: An eighty-one-year-old man pulling a book off a shelf and dropping it on a table.

PHOOLCHAND: Two things, and then I am going to ask you to leave and never say my name in Barkheri. One.

SFX: Pages.

PHOOLCHAND: A turn of worship is immovable property. Not a job. Not a favour. Property, like a field. That means a paper that gives it away must be registered, exactly like a field, and a paper that is not registered cannot be looked at by any court in this country. Cannot be read. Cannot be produced. Does not exist.

KESAR: This paper.

PHOOLCHAND: This paper.

SFX: Nothing.

KESAR: And two?

PHOOLCHAND: (and here his voice does something strange — it gets younger) Two is worse. Two is what I have not slept about since Monday. A turn of worship cannot be sold at all. Not registered, not unregistered, not for eleven hundred rupees, not for eleven lakh. The office and the god cannot be bought for money by anybody. The man who buys it buys nothing. He has always bought nothing.

SFX: Nothing. Very long.

KESAR: Sahib. How long have you known that?

PHOOLCHAND: (a beat) Since about nineteen seventy-four.

SFX: A chair.

PHOOLCHAND: Go away, girl. Take the copy, not the original — no, take it, I've written it out fair, it's on the table, it took me all night and my hand is not what it was. Go away and don't come back and don't say my name.

NARRATOR: She was on the five past five bus before the compressor stopped. Fifty-one years he had known, and every one of those years he had drafted the next paper for the same house. Remember that. It is going to matter on the last morning.

SFX: Cut. Barkheri. A courtyard, midday, the committee, thirty people.

JORAWAR: — and so the re-cut stands as read. Any objection is in writing, registered, by the full moon. Girdhari, next item.

TEJMAL: I have an objection.

SFX: Thirty people turning.

JORAWAR: You have sixty-one days and a grievance about rain, Tejmal. Sit down.

TEJMAL: The twenty-ninth of Chaitra is Thursday.

JORAWAR: It is.

TEJMAL: And in your block.

JORAWAR: In my block since sixty-eight, as the book says, as Girdhari read out, as everybody now knows, thank you.

TEJMAL: (a breath) I'm asking the committee to leave that morning open.

SFX: Nothing.

JORAWAR: Open.

TEJMAL: Nobody stands. Nobody takes the box. Nine days, hold them open till the full moon, and let whoever proves it take the money afterwards.

JORAWAR: (pleasantly) What a good idea that is. And who feeds the god on Thursday?

TEJMAL: Somebody who has fed him every morning for fifteen years without being asked.

SFX: Thirty people making one sound.

JORAWAR: Brother. Look at me. Am I angry?

TEJMAL: I don't care.

JORAWAR: (and for the first time in nine episodes there is a pause before he speaks) ...No. Put it to the committee. Hands.

SFX: A long, shuffling, cowardly silence. Then one hand. Then another. Then a lot.

RUKMANI: (writing) Twelve for. Nine against. Four sitting on their hands, and I am writing down which four.

JORAWAR: Fine. Thursday is open. It changes nothing. An open morning is not a claim.

NARRATOR: He was right about that. It was not a claim. It was worse, and he did not see it, because he had never in his life had to think about what a thing sounds like.

SFX: Cut. Black. Rope through an iron ring. Slow, this time. Deliberate.

SFX: Village night, the very end of it. Two hundred people not making a sound, which is the loudest thing in this episode.

CHHOTU: (a whisper) Didi. There's — didi, the whole road. The whole road is here.

KESAR: How many?

CHHOTU: I can't count them.

KESAR: Count them.

CHHOTU: (a beat) Two hundred? More. Second bell. Didi, there's people from the Jhilan house, there's Narmada mai, she came on the night bus, she's at the front, she's —

KESAR: Where's my mother?

SFX: Nothing.

CHHOTU: ...At the back, didi.

SFX: Nothing. Long.

KESAR: Ninety-one lamps. Three of them are cracked and I have told them four times.

SFX: A rope pulled hand over hand. One stroke.

SFX: The bell. Iron, cracked at the lip, out over the fields, for a long time.

SFX: Two hundred people, still not moving.

SFX: A second stroke. A third.

NARRATOR: She rang it the way she had rung it for fifteen years, which is to say she rang it correctly, and the whole village stood in the dark and heard the difference.

SFX: Feet on stone. One pair, coming through the crowd.

JORAWAR: Get off the rope.

KESAR: The morning's open. The committee voted.

JORAWAR: The committee voted that nobody stands. You are standing.

KESAR: I'm not standing. I'm ringing.

JORAWAR: Get off the rope or I will have you taken off it, and this time there will be two hundred people and no fair to blame it on.

SFX: The rope going still. Nothing. Long.

TEJMAL: (from the crowd, and his voice carries badly, and he says it anyway) Leave her.

SFX: Two hundred people.

JORAWAR: Brother —

TEJMAL: I said leave her. Get away from the rope. You too. Both of you, get away from it.

SFX: Feet. A man walking to the front and standing next to a sweeper.

NARRATOR: He was not doing it for her. He had forty days a year under water and a grievance about rain and a brother who had told him to sit down in front of thirty people. Motives are not the business. What the village heard is the business.

JORAWAR: Tejmal. Think about the monsoon.

TEJMAL: I have thought about nothing else since nineteen ninety-one.

SFX: Nothing.

JORAWAR: (very quietly) Rukmani. Close the day-book.

RUKMANI: The day-book closes at first light, sahib. It's not first light.

SFX: Nothing.

JORAWAR: Rukmani.

RUKMANI: (and she says it in the voice she uses for reading back numbers, which is why nobody can argue with it) I write what happens, sahib. That's the whole of my job and you gave it to me.

TEJMAL: Then write it.

RUKMANI: Write what, sahib. Give me the words. I don't invent words.

SFX: Nothing. Long. Two hundred people waiting for a man to say a sentence.

TEJMAL: (loud, flat, to the committee, to the crowd, to his brother) Twenty-ninth of Chaitra. First bell rung at four. Not by the block. Not by me. Kesar's ninth — the first of Mohari's nine days — and the box on this morning is hers.

SFX: A pen. A cheap ballpoint on cheap paper, and in a silence like that you can hear every letter.

RUKMANI: (reading back exactly what she has written, because that is what she does) "Twenty-ninth Chaitra. Bell at four. Kesar's ninth. Box hers."
