# EPISODE 6 — "Say It in Front of Him"

SFX: A sheet of stiff town paper being taken out of a girl's hands. Not snatched. Taken, slowly, by two fingers.

JORAWAR: The fourteenth of Magh. Well remembered. You read a date the way your grandmother read a date.

KESAR: What did she sign nineteen years ago?

JORAWAR: Ask her.

KESAR: I'm asking you, in her house, with the door open.

JORAWAR: (folding the paper, twice, unhurried) And I'm telling you to ask her, in her house, with the door open. Bhanwari. The thumb, or not the thumb?

SFX: Nothing. Long. Then a woman's chair scraping back — away from the table.

JORAWAR: Not tonight, then. Fine. There's time. There's twenty-nine days.

SFX: Sagwan door. Footsteps down the lane, unhurried, and a lane full of neighbours pretending to sweep.

NARRATOR: He walked out of that house without the thumbprint he came for, and he did not mind, because he had come for something else and got it. Now Kesar knew there was a second paper. Now she had to find out what was on it. And a person looking for one paper does not notice the man walking her towards a different one.

SFX: Cut. Dawn. Wet stone. A broom. The four o'clock bell, still rung flat.

CHHOTU: Didi. Didi. Tejmal sahib is at the storeroom and he's shouting.

KESAR: Shouting about what?

CHHOTU: Rain.

KESAR: (a beat) Say that again.

CHHOTU: He's shouting about the rain. He does it every year. Second bell — didi, I'm not supposed to talk to you —

SFX: A girl already walking.

SFX: Storeroom door. An angry middle-aged man mid-sentence.

TEJMAL: — sixty-one days and forty of them are under water. Forty! Nobody comes! You cannot put a hundred rupees in a box you cannot reach because the road is a river. And he sits on eleven days of Chaitra and calls it fair.

GIRDHARI: The book says what the book says, Tejmal sahib.

TEJMAL: The book says my father took what he was given and shut up. Fine. Write it. Write "Tejmal, mud."

KESAR: Sahib.

SFX: A room going quiet.

TEJMAL: (immediately, tiredly) Not you.

KESAR: Two fair days.

TEJMAL: What?

KESAR: Twenty-ninth of Chaitra to the seventh of Vaisakh. Nine days. Two of them are fair days. One lakh forty each.

SFX: A chair.

TEJMAL: Who told you that number?

KESAR: My mother. She's known it for nineteen years.

TEJMAL: (slowly) And why are you saying it to me?

KESAR: Because those nine days sit inside manager sahib's block and they are not his, and if they come out of his block at the re-cut, they have to go somewhere. Somebody gets them. It could be me. It could be you. It cannot be him.

SFX: Nothing. Long.

TEJMAL: Girdhari. Is she right?

GIRDHARI: (carefully) I'm the writer, sahib. I don't have an opinion.

KESAR: Kaka. Open sixty-eight.

GIRDHARI: No.

KESAR: Open sixty-eight.

GIRDHARI: Child, I have forty years in this room —

KESAR: And in twenty-nine days there's a re-cut, and manager sahib has promised you that you'll read the new roll out to the whole village. Yes? He promised you that at his gate. In the heat. When you told him my date.

SFX: Nothing. Long.

GIRDHARI: ...How do you know he said that.

KESAR: I didn't. You just told me.

SFX: An old man sitting down very suddenly.

NARRATOR: She had guessed. She had been guessing for four days and she guessed out loud in front of a man who had a grievance about rain. Sometimes that is all a reversal is.

KESAR: Kaka, listen to me. There's a woman from Jhilan buying days. She has a list. On her list, those nine days have no name against them, and she has already asked me who in this village can read the old hand. If you won't read it, she brings her own man from town, and he reads your book out loud at the re-cut instead of you, and every man in Barkheri watches a stranger do the one thing you can do.

GIRDHARI: That is a cruel thing to say to me.

KESAR: Yes.

TEJMAL: Open it, Girdhari.

GIRDHARI: Sahib —

TEJMAL: Rukmani! Get Rukmani, she writes the day-book, she should hear it. And Kanhaiya, and the two from the Jhilan house, they're here for the oil accounts. Get them all in.

SFX: Feet. A room filling — six, seven, eight people, benches dragged, the door propped.

RUKMANI: What am I writing?

TEJMAL: Whatever he reads.

SFX: The cloth-bound register. Pages, thick and slow. Stopping.

GIRDHARI: (and his voice has changed — this is the reading voice, forty years old) Nineteen sixty-seven. Twenty-ninth Chaitra to seventh Vaisakh. Nine days. Held whole, not by turn. Holder — Mohari, widow.

SFX: Somebody's breath. Rukmani's pen.

GIRDHARI: Nineteen sixty-eight. Same nine days. And here the writer's hand changes. Entry reads — "the said nine days having been sold by the said widow to Bhairon Singh son of —" I will not read the father's name, sahib, he's your grandfather, you know it. "— sold, for a consideration of rupees eleven hundred only, and thereafter merged in his block."

SFX: Nothing. Very long.

TEJMAL: Eleven hundred.

GIRDHARI: Eleven hundred.

TEJMAL: For nine days. For two fair days.

GIRDHARI: In sixty-eight, sahib, eleven hundred rupees was a buffalo and a half.

RUKMANI: (writing, and reading back exactly what she has written, because that is what she does) "Nine days. Sold. Eleven hundred rupees. Nineteen sixty-eight."

KANHAIYA: (into the silence, oily) Well — sold is sold, isn't it. Nothing to argue with. Sold is sold.

SFX: Nothing.

TEJMAL: Shut up, Kanhaiya.

NARRATOR: Fifty-seven years. Every fair morning of those fifty-seven years, one man's family had stood under that bell on those two days, and the whole village had watched them do it, and not one person in the whole village had said the word out loud. It took a sweeper eleven days.

KESAR: (very quiet) Kaka. Say her name again.

GIRDHARI: I've read it. It's read.

KESAR: Say it to him. Not to me. To him.

SFX: Nothing. Long. An old man closing a register and not letting go of it.

GIRDHARI: Tejmal sahib. Her name was Mohari. Mohari, widow, of this village, who swept this courtyard the way her daughter-in-law sweeps it and the way this girl sweeps it.

TEJMAL: I know who she was.

GIRDHARI: Then hear it properly, sahib, because I have written this book for forty years and I have kept my mouth shut in it for nineteen. Nine days. Twenty-ninth of Chaitra to the seventh of Vaisakh. And they were hers.
