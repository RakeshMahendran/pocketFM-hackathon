# EPISODE 11 — "Four Lakh Sixty"

SFX: A government form being folded, twice, by hands that are not steady. A lamp.

BHANWARI: Now you know why I asked him not to open sixty-eight.

KESAR: No. No, I don't. Sixty-eight is Mohari. This is me. They're two different papers.

BHANWARI: (tired) Girl. Think for one minute like a man with a jeep. What do you want from Mohari's nine days?

KESAR: They come to her son. Her son's dead. So they come to me.

BHANWARI: Through what?

SFX: Nothing.

BHANWARI: Through your father. Through her son. That's the only door, Kesar. And nineteen years ago a man walked into this house with a stamp pad and quietly took the door out of the wall.

SFX: Nothing. Very long.

KESAR: On paper I'm not her granddaughter.

BHANWARI: On paper you are Tejmal's bastard.

SFX: Nothing.

BHANWARI: And now you have made the whole village love you and a man has stood next to you at the rope, and that man's name is on the paper, and when they open it — and they will open it, at the re-cut, in front of everybody — it will not look like something done to you nineteen years ago. It will look like the reason he stood next to you.

NARRATOR: She had climbed for eleven days. She had a book entry, a village, a brother of the enemy, and three thousand one hundred and fifty-one rupees. She had, on that night, exactly one thing more than she had in episode one, and it was the knowledge of how far she had left to fall.

SFX: Cut. Morning. Rain on a tin roof — the first rain, early, everyone talking about how early it is. A tyre shop compressor underneath it.

SFX: Heels on concrete stairs. A different stairwell. An office with air conditioning.

SITARA: Sit, ji. Not that chair, that one, the other has files. Tea?

KESAR: No.

SITARA: Have tea. You look like a person who has been awake.

SFX: A cup and saucer.

KESAR: Sitara ji. I need to know what a birth entry does to a claim.

SITARA: (a pause; the sound of a woman putting down a pen) ...Say that again slowly.

KESAR: If a paper says my father is somebody who isn't my father, and my claim comes through my father —

SITARA: Then you have no claim. You have a very good story and no claim. Who is on it?

KESAR: Tejmal.

SFX: Nothing. Long. Rain.

SITARA: (and her voice has gone completely flat) Tejmal. The man who stood next to you at the rope in front of two hundred people. The man the whole district is talking about.

KESAR: Yes.

SITARA: Ji. Do you know what that looks like from a room in Jhilan?

KESAR: It looks like I'm his and he was helping me.

SITARA: It looks like the two of you invented Mohari's nine days between you to break his brother's block. That is exactly what it looks like, and it is what Jorawar ji will say, and I am telling you plainly because I am about to do something to you and I would like you to see it coming.

SFX: A chair. A drawer.

KESAR: Sitara ji —

SITARA: How much do you think I have in this circuit?

KESAR: You said eleven days.

SITARA: I said eleven days. I did not say the number. Nobody in Jhilan says the number.

SFX: A ledger. A small, private, hard-backed book, opened.

SITARA: Two years I have been buying into Barkheri. Small holders. The Jhilan house, who never come. Kanhaiya's father-in-law. Old men who want money now instead of a fair day in Vaisakh. Eleven days, and I paid for every one of them in cash, in Jorawar ji's office, with Jorawar ji counting.

KESAR: Why cash?

SITARA: Because a turn is not a thing a bank will lend on and because the men selling it did not want their brothers to know. Why does anybody do anything in cash, ji.

SFX: The book closed.

SITARA: And then a sweeper girl goes to an eighty-one-year-old vakil and comes back saying a turn of worship cannot be sold for money. At all. To anybody. Ever.

SFX: Nothing.

KESAR: It can't.

SITARA: I know it can't. I checked on Thursday. I have had a man in Jaipur reading judgments since Thursday and he stopped ringing me on Saturday because he did not want to be the one to say it.

SFX: Rain on the roof.

SITARA: So let us both be very clear about where we are standing. If your rule is wrong, you lose your nine days. If your rule is right, I lose eleven days that I have already paid for, and the money is gone into a man who counted it in front of me and wrote nothing down.

KESAR: Then you should be on my side.

SITARA: (a short, real laugh) Ji. That is the stupidest sentence you have said in four weeks. There are no sides. There is a full moon in six days.

SFX: A pen picked up.

SITARA: Six days. Here is what happens in six.

KESAR: Sitara ji —

SITARA: Six days. Either the sub-registrar at Jhilan puts the rule into his register — in writing, in the book, in Hukum sahib's hand, where it becomes a thing that has happened and not a thing an old man said in a room — or he doesn't.

KESAR: And if he doesn't?

SITARA: If he doesn't, then on the morning of the full moon I stand up in that courtyard and I say that the entry Girdhari read out was shown to me by you before it was read, and that you had been in that storeroom alone at night, twice, which you have, and that I am a businesswoman who was misled by a village girl and a man with a grievance about rain.

SFX: Nothing. Very long.

KESAR: You'd say I forged the book.

SITARA: I would say I was misled. Ji, look at me. I like you. You are the first person in that village who told me a true number without being asked twice. And I have four lakh sixty thousand rupees of my own money standing in that circuit and no receipt for one paisa of it, so please understand what I am and what I am not.

KESAR: (very quietly) Four lakh sixty.

SITARA: Four lakh sixty thousand. Eleven days. Cash.

SFX: A cup set down.

SITARA: Now finish the tea, and go and find out what Hukum sahib is frightened of, because it is not the law, and it is not Jorawar ji. Somebody in Jhilan knows. Find out by Tuesday.

NARRATOR: She did find out by Tuesday. And what the sub-registrar of Jhilan was frightened of turned out to be the same thing everybody in this story has been frightened of, which is his own signature.

SFX: Cut. Barkheri. Night. The storeroom. One lamp. An old man not looking up from a register.

GIRDHARI: You've been to Jhilan.

KESAR: Kaka, I need every entry Sitara ji's eleven days came out of. Names, dates, which block.

GIRDHARI: Why.

KESAR: Because she paid four lakh sixty thousand rupees in cash for them.

SFX: A pen stopping.

GIRDHARI: ...Say the number again.

KESAR: Four lakh sixty thousand. Counted in manager sahib's office. On his table. In his hands.

GIRDHARI: (and there is something in his voice that has not been there in eleven episodes) Child. Sit down.

KESAR: Kaka?

GIRDHARI: Four lakh sixty thousand rupees came into this village and not one rupee of it is in my book, or in Rukmani's day-book, or in the shrine accounts I read out to two hundred people every Vaisakh. Where is the receipt?

SFX: Nothing. Long.

KESAR: There is no receipt. Sitara ji has paid Jorawar four lakh sixty thousand rupees in cash for eleven days of that god's year, and she does not hold one piece of paper for any of it.
