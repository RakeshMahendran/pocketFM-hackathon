# EPISODE 3 — "Forty Rupees a Day"

SFX: A wall. A girl's back against it, sliding down it, slowly, into gravel.

KESAR: (under her breath, flat) Sixty-eight. Sixty-eight page. Sixty-eight page.

SFX: The iron gate closing. Bhanwari's slippers on the road, coming. Kesar getting up fast and going the other way.

NARRATOR: She did not ask her mother what it meant. She had just heard her mother trade her away for it, and there is a kind of shame that makes a person go quiet for three days. She went quiet for three days.

SFX: Morning. The shrine courtyard, empty of fair now. A broom on stone, that dry sweeping hiss, and under it — from far off — the four o'clock bell, rung badly by someone else.

CHHOTU: He rings it flat. Sahib's nephew. He rings it flat and nobody says anything.

KESAR: He's holding the rope too high.

CHHOTU: I told him.

KESAR: You didn't.

CHHOTU: ...I didn't.

SFX: Sweeping.

CHHOTU: Didi. If you're not on the board, why are you sweeping?

KESAR: Ninety-one lamps.

CHHOTU: That's not an answer.

KESAR: Somebody has to fill ninety-one lamps.

SFX: A car. A real car, not a jeep — town suspension, a horn used once, politely — coming up a track that has never had one on it.

CHHOTU: (awed) Second bell. Second bell. Didi, who —

SFX: A door. Heels on stone, which is a sound this courtyard has never made.

SITARA: Namaste ji. Whose is this bell?

KESAR: The god's.

SITARA: (a short laugh, genuine) Correct answer. Wrong question, my fault. On the twelfth of next month — whose is it?

KESAR: Manager sahib's.

SITARA: Jorawar ji. Yes. And on the thirteenth?

KESAR: Tejmal sahib. Monsoon days are Tejmal sahib's, mostly.

SITARA: And on the fourteenth, fifteenth, sixteenth?

SFX: Sweeping stops.

KESAR: ...You want the roll.

SITARA: I want the roll, ji. Somebody in this village must be able to read the roll off the board and tell me who actually stands under that bell each morning, because I promise you the board and the truth are two different documents. Understand?

KESAR: Girdhari keeps the book.

SITARA: Girdhari ji is a gentleman and gentlemen take three weeks. I have eleven days. Who are you?

KESAR: Nobody. I sweep.

SITARA: You knew the twelfth, the thirteenth and you were about to know the fourteenth. Nobody doesn't know that. Walk with me. Five days. Forty rupees a day.

SFX: Nothing. Long.

SITARA: Ji? Forty a day. Is it too little? It's too little. Fifty.

KESAR: (very carefully) Forty.

SITARA: Forty, then. Done.

NARRATOR: Fifteen years. Ninety-one lamps a night, five thousand and some mornings on that rope, and this was the first time anyone in Barkheri had ever put a number on her.

SFX: Paper. A folded list, opened, the sound of town stationery.

SITARA: Start at the first of Chaitra. Read.

KESAR: First to eleventh — manager sahib. Twelfth — manager sahib. Thirteenth to nineteenth — Tejmal sahib.

SITARA: (writing) He gets the wet ones. Poor man.

KESAR: Twentieth, twenty-first — the Jhilan house, they don't come, they send money to Girdhari and Girdhari does it.

SITARA: They send money for it? Write that down, that one's worth something. Go on.

KESAR: Twenty-second to twenty-eighth — Kanhaiya does the standing but the days belong to sahib's daughter-in-law.

SITARA: Rukmani ji. Fine. Go on.

KESAR: Ji. Why are you writing down who actually stands, and not who owns it?

SITARA: Because in a village those are two different men and only one of them will sell to me. Understand? A man who owns a day and never comes to it has already decided what the day is worth to him. Go on.

SFX: A page turning.

KESAR: Twenty-ninth.

SITARA: Yes?

KESAR: Twenty-ninth to the seventh of Vaisakh.

SITARA: That's nine days, ji. Whose?

SFX: Nothing.

KESAR: The board doesn't say.

SITARA: The board says something. Boards always say something.

KESAR: The board has a line ruled through it. It's an old line. It's cut into the wood, not chalked.

SITARA: (a change in her voice — small, professional, hungry) Ji. Come here. Look at my list. Not that column, this column. What do you see?

KESAR: Your writing.

SITARA: Against every set of days, what do you see?

KESAR: A name.

SITARA: And against those nine?

SFX: Paper, held out. Wind moving it.

SITARA: Nine days of that god's year, ji. Fair days, two of them. And on my list, and on your board, and — I will bet you forty rupees — in Girdhari ji's book, there is not one man's name written against them.
