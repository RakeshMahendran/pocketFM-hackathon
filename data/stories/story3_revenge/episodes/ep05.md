# Episode 5 — "The Price of a Seat"

SFX: A diesel engine idling at the roadside, ticking under the bonnet. A tarpaulin being untied, one knot at a time.

MAQSOOD: Sit in the cab or stand in the sun. Standing is free.

BIRJU: I'll stand.

MAQSOOD: Everyone says that. Nobody means it. What do you want, munshi?

BIRJU: Three people to Kaimur. Or to anywhere on your route that is not this district.

MAQSOOD: Three heads. When?

BIRJU: Before the next pay-out.

SFX: A pencil on the back of a cigarette packet. Very short strokes.

MAQSOOD: Diesel is one thing, but you are not asking me about diesel. Three heads out of teghra more, at night, before a pay-out. That is not transport, that is a transfer, and a transfer has a price for everybody standing near it. Two thousand a head to me.

BIRJU: Six thousand. I can find six thousand.

MAQSOOD: You have not let me finish. Two thousand a head to me. That is the cheap part. Then there is the carrying.

BIRJU: The carrying.

MAQSOOD: The number goes with them.

SFX: The tarpaulin, dropping.

BIRJU: What?

MAQSOOD: Munshi, you are the one who writes it, and you are asking me? The number is on the house. The house is three people. Three people get into my truck, the number gets into my truck. I hand them to a kiln in Kaimur and I hand the kiln a slip with the figure on it, and the kiln pays your Dhaniram, and now the family owes Kaimur. Plus the transfer. Which is fifteen per cent, and I do not set that, that is what everyone charges, ask anyone.

BIRJU: (slowly) So they would arrive owing more than they left owing.

MAQSOOD: They would arrive owing eighty-nine four hundred plus fifteen. Call it one lakh two thousand, eight hundred and ten. In a district where they know nobody, at a kiln where the wage is lower because it is nearer the crusher. Also the water is bad there. I mention the water because people do not think about water and then they are surprised.

BIRJU: And if they simply walk. At night. Across the fields.

SFX: Maqsood laughing — one breath out through the nose.

MAQSOOD: Then they are three people walking across fields with nothing, and the number stays here, in a book, with their names on it. And a number does not get tired, munshi. It waits. It waits eleven years if it has to. Then one of them comes back for a funeral — they always come back for a funeral — and it is standing at the crossing.

BIRJU: You've seen that.

MAQSOOD: I have driven that. Twice. I do not enjoy it. That is why I take fifteen per cent — because on the day the officers come, the man holding the family is the man standing next to the truck, and that is me, and I would like to be paid for standing there.

SFX: The cab door. The engine catching properly, blue and loud.

MAQSOOD: Get me six thousand and a signature and I will do it. Not your signature. A signature that is worth something.

BIRJU: Whose signature is worth something?

MAQSOOD: (over the engine) The one on the book, munshi. Whoever's hand the book is in. That is the only signature there is out here.

SFX: A truck pulling away. Gravel spitting. Then the road, empty, and a boy standing in it.

NARRATOR: He walked back the four kilometres and did not notice doing it. He had gone out that morning believing there was a door, and the man at the door had been perfectly polite, and had priced the door, and the price of the door was the door.

SFX: The office. The fan with the bent blade. The almirah, still locked — he tries it now out of habit, the way you touch a bruise.

BIRJU: (to himself) Fifteen per cent. One lakh two thousand eight hundred and ten.

SFX: The new book opening. Pages — he has filled sixteen of them in nine weeks.

NARRATOR: He had decided, on the road, to do the only thing he could still do. He would learn the book. Not read it — learn it. Every entry he had made himself since Chait, in his own hand, from page one, so that at least one section of it would be a thing he could stand behind.

SFX: Pages. A pen tapping the table between them. Pages.

BIRJU: Page four. Chhotu, ten thousand, hen. Page seven, Sanichari, interest on the Aghan fine — interest on a fine —

SFX: The pen stops tapping.

BIRJU: Page eleven. Lakhan's hands. Two thousand.

SFX: A page turning. Then nothing at all for a long moment.

BIRJU: Page twelve.

SFX: Nothing. Long.

NARRATOR: Nine days ago Dhaniram had sat him down with a fresh nib and told him that the format of an advance entry is not obvious and must be practised, because a badly written advance entry is the one thing an inspector notices. He had dictated four practice lines. Birju had written all four in his best hand, and had been pleased, because his best hand is very good.

DHANIRAM: (recorded in memory, warm, unhurried) Name first. Then the head — advance against salary. Then the figure. Then the date. Do it properly, beta, and I will not have to teach you twice.

SFX: The page again. A fingertip moving along a line, dragging slightly on the paper.

BIRJU: (reading his own handwriting) "Birju. Advance against salary. Four thousand. Tuesday."

SFX: Nothing.

BIRJU: (again, flatly) Four thousand.

NARRATOR: It was not a practice line. There is no practice book at teghra more and there never has been. The entry is in the current book, on page twelve, in ink, between Lakhan's fine and a delivery of coal — and it is signed, in the space at the end where the munshi signs to certify that the entry is true.

NARRATOR: The signature is his. He wrote it himself, on Tuesday, and he was pleased with it.
