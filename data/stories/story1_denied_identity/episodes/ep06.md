# Episode 6 — "Eleven Sentences"

SFX: A cowshed at night. A buffalo shifting her weight, hoof, straw, hoof. A hurricane lamp turned up, the wick catching.

CHAITRA: I have the photograph.

MALLESHA: Put it where the lamp is not. Kerosene and old prints.

CHAITRA: You're holding the garland.

MALLESHA: Sit on the sack, not the step. The step is wet.

SFX: A jute sack. A girl sitting down on it, hard.

CHAITRA: Appa. Ratnamma is sleeping in my room. Amma made her a bed and did not say one word while she made it, and Prakash is sitting outside the door because he thinks somebody has to. So the house already knows. Whatever you are protecting, it is finished. Talk.

MALLESHA: (nothing)

CHAITRA: Then I'll count. Eleven sentences. That's what you gave the fourth standard for the essay — eleven sentences on My Village, no more, because a boy who is allowed twelve will write thirty and say nothing. Eleven sentences, Appa. Begin.

SFX: Nothing. Long. The buffalo. Straw.

NARRATOR: He had made four hundred children write eleven sentences a year for twenty-four years. He had never once had to do it himself.

MALLESHA: One. There was an elder brother, and he was the one the family put its money on, and that was correct, because he was better than the rest of it.

SFX: The lamp hissing.

MALLESHA: Two. The order came in June of ninety-four, assistant teacher, and the house was mortgaged for thirty-eight thousand rupees to the Nerlige society for the marriage and the sweets and the bus fare, because everyone said the salary would clear it in two years.

CHAITRA: Three.

MALLESHA: Three. On the twenty-seventh of July there was a lorry at the Hosakere junction, and on the fourteenth of August there was a death, and the joining report had not been given, so there was no service, and no service means no gratuity, no family pension, no compassionate appointment — nothing at all comes to a house where the man died eighteen days before he became a government servant.

SFX: A hoof. Straw.

MALLESHA: Four. There was a younger brother in Bhiwandi with a seventh standard pass and no English, four hundred rupees a week, and by September the society had put a notice on the door of the house.

MALLESHA: Five. A man from the block office came to the house in the second week of September with the file still in his hand, and he said the joining report can be given late, thirty days is a rule and rules have a date column, and the date column is written by a clerk.

CHAITRA: (barely audible) Thammanna.

MALLESHA: Six. He said the photograph on the order is half an inch square and it is a photograph of a young man with a moustache, and there are two young men with moustaches in that family, and one of them is on a pyre and one of them is standing in this room.

SFX: Nothing. Long.

MALLESHA: Seven. On the fourth of November nineteen ninety-four a joining report was signed, and the hand that signed it was told to sign it slower than it wanted to, because it was not that hand's name.

MALLESHA: Eight. Every seventh of every month since then, twelve hundred rupees has left this house, and no month has been missed, not the month your grandmother died and not the month you were born.

CHAITRA: Nine.

MALLESHA: Nine. The seventh standard was completed at Nerlige, and after that everything — the training certificate, the refresher courses, the district level workshop on multiplication methods in two thousand and nine, the second prize — all of it was earned by working, and none of it was earned by that name.

SFX: His voice does not change. That is the thing about it. It does not change once.

MALLESHA: Ten. Four hundred children a year for twenty-four years is nine thousand six hundred children, and the arithmetic of this district is in their hands, and that was the payment, and it was accepted as the payment.

SFX: Nothing. Long.

CHAITRA: Eleven, Appa.

MALLESHA: Eleven. There is no eleventh sentence. That is all.

SFX: The lamp. The buffalo. A girl breathing badly.

CHAITRA: (and it breaks out of her) Twenty-two years. Twenty-two years I have told people my father is a coward. Do you know that? When Girija teacher's husband got the felicitation shawl at the taluk function and you sat in the third row and would not go on the stage — I was in eighth standard and I told my whole class that my father is a man who is frightened of a microphone. When the school photograph came and you were the only teacher standing behind the pillar — I told them. When Harsha's family asked for a photo of the family and Amma sent one with you cut out at the shoulder — I said, that is my father, he does not like his face. I have been ashamed of you since I was eight years old and it was the wrong shame. It was the wrong shame, Appa, and you let me carry it.

MALLESHA: (nothing)

CHAITRA: Say something!

MALLESHA: (very quietly) A photograph goes in the newspaper. A newspaper goes to the block office. That is all.

SFX: Nothing. Long.

CHAITRA: (steadier) Then we go tomorrow. Both of us. To the block office, to the deputy director, to the police if it comes to that. You give it back. The name, the post, everything. It's twenty-four years, they'll take it out of you in some way, but they'll take it and it will be finished, and then I go to counter four with a clean paper and I get verified like a normal girl and I stop being the daughter of a dead man—

SFX: A steel bucket knocked over. Set upright.

MALLESHA: No.

CHAITRA: You don't get to say no. It's my file.

MALLESHA: It is not your file.

CHAITRA: It is my file, Appa, my name, my—

MALLESHA: There is a tin trunk under the cot in your mother's room.

CHAITRA: I opened it. I opened it on Tuesday.

MALLESHA: There is a cloth pouch under the sari receipts. It has not been opened since two thousand.

SFX: Feet, fast, out of the shed. Across a yard. A door. A trunk on cement. Cloth. Paper.

NARRATOR: A birth certificate has a column. It is column four. It does not say father. It says name of father, and under it, in the registrar's hand, in violet ink, a name.

SFX: Paper. Nothing else.

CHAITRA: (from inside the house, and it does not sound like a shout, it sounds like something coming loose) Appa.

SFX: Chappals crossing the yard. Slowly.

CHAITRA: My birth certificate says Lokanath.

MALLESHA: (from the doorway) Yes.

CHAITRA: Registered fourteenth of January, two thousand. Name of father — Lokanath. Which means every record this state has ever made of me — school admission, transfer certificate, caste and income, the seven-year study certificate, the hall ticket I wrote the exam with, all of it, all of it, every single one — makes me the daughter of a man who was dead five years before I was born.
