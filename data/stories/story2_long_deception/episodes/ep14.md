# EPISODE 14 — "The Fifth"

SFX: Four in the morning. A verandah. A tin box being opened — the family one, the one that held sweets.

RUPA: Count it.

HARIYA: Beta—

RUPA: Count it, Baba. Out loud. Everything in this house gets counted in front of somebody, I said it to you on the day of my first salary and you said it back to me.

SFX: Notes. In fives. It takes a long time.

HARIYA: Ninety thousand.

RUPA: One rupee in ten of everything that register earned, for six months, in a box under a bed. I never spent one note of it and I could not have told you why, and now I know why.

HARIYA: I will come with you to Lakhan.

RUPA: You will come with me to the tea shop first.

SFX: Nothing.

HARIYA: The tea shop.

RUPA: It opens at half past five. Sukhdev is there at half past five. The two brothers from the mill are there. The messenger with the cycle is there, and whatever is said at that bench is in Kachhwara by nine and at the fair by Sunday, and you know that, because you and I have both been the thing that was said there.

HARIYA: What do you want me to say?

RUPA: The ninth of the eighth month. The thumb. The witness. And that the notice for the posts was published on the second of October.

HARIYA: They will—

RUPA: They will. And then it will be over, and it will be over because you said it and not because somebody found it out. Baba, I have spent nine months learning exactly one thing, and it is this: the paper is nothing. The reading of it out loud in front of people is everything.

SFX: A tea shop. A kettle. Glass on a wooden bench. Four men, and the sound stops the moment somebody stands up who was not expected to stand up.

HARIYA: I want to say something and I am going to say all of it, so nobody has to ask me a second question afterwards.

SFX: Nothing. Long. The kettle only.

HARIYA: On the ninth of the eighth month last year I put my thumb in a book above the bus stand for a constable's post for my daughter. Ninety thousand rupees. The recruitment notice for that post was published on the second of October. I paid seven weeks before there was anything to pay for, and I did not tell her, and when she came back from the ground with the pencil line through her number I let her think it was her running and her marks.

SFX: A glass being set down on wood, very carefully, by somebody.

HARIYA: And in March I mortgaged the house to Lakhan to make good on the thumb. And I told this bench that it was a loan for a tractor.

SUKHDEV: Hariya.

HARIYA: I have not finished. In February I took forty rupees from Sukhdev twice for her bus fare and I have not returned it and I am returning it now.

SFX: Coins on wood.

SUKHDEV: Sit down, you fool.

HARIYA: I would rather stand for this part.

SFX: A scooter at a gate. Idling. A ledger against a petrol tank.

LAKHAN: Fifth. Ninety thousand, principal. Have you got it or have you got a story?

RUPA: Switch it off.

LAKHAN: What?

RUPA: You are going to have to count ninety thousand rupees and write a receipt and hand back a paper, and you cannot do that sitting on a scooter with the engine running. Switch it off and get down.

SFX: Nothing. Long. Then the engine, dying. Then a man's weight coming off a scooter onto gravel.

NARRATOR: In nineteen years he had got down for Sukhdev twice, and then he had stopped getting down for anybody. Remember that. It was the only thing in this story that ever happened quietly.

SFX: Notes. In fives, on the seat of a scooter. All the way to the end.

LAKHAN: Ninety.

RUPA: Now the paper. And the receipt for the paper. And you will read the receipt out loud, because you are a written man and there are four people standing at that gate.

LAKHAN: Received ninety thousand in full satisfaction of principal. Property paper of the house and front land at Chandpura, returned this day to the hand of Hariya, night watchman, and there is nothing more owing on it from this family to me, ever.

MUNNI: Say the date.

LAKHAN: The fifth.

SFX: Seven in the morning. A different building. A gate, a rifle rack, a chair being dragged. Stone.

TAPAN: Madam ji — Sir said seven, Sir said if anyone makes you stand—

RUPA: Nobody made me stand. Tapan, take one of these, they are heavy.

SFX: Four registers set down on a table. Then a soft-covered book. Then a school notebook with a peeling cover.

KISHORE: What is this.

RUPA: Four registers. Four hundred and nine matters. Bansi's advance book, twenty-two names, countersigned by him in his own yard. The receiving file — eighty-one of them are Dularchand's. And this one is a receipt book that goes back three years, of money taken for posts that do not exist, in a hand that will sign a statement to that effect at eleven o'clock this morning.

KISHORE: And?

RUPA: And a statement from me. Written last night, in my hand, unsigned, so that you can watch me sign it.

KISHORE: Read me the third line.

RUPA: "From the fourth morning of the third month I knew that the office had no existence in law, and I continued to write in the register, and I put my signature on three hundred and one files after that date, and I did it because I was told that the alternative was that four hundred and nine people would lose everything they had, and because twenty-eight thousand rupees is twenty-eight thousand rupees, and both of those were true at the same time."

SFX: Nothing. Long.

KISHORE: Do you know what happens to you now?

RUPA: Some of it.

KISHORE: Some of it. Madam, there is one thing in your favour and it is not your conscience, so do not look at me like that. It is that column. Four hundred and nine matters with the complainant read back, thumbed, witnessed by a different person each time, dated, and served with a receiving. That is not paper. That is a case file. I can take four hundred and nine of those into this station's register this week and every one of them survives, because every one of them can be proved to have been said, on a date, in front of a person. If you had done it any other way it would all be firewood.

RUPA: Sir, the lorry for Patna is tomorrow.

KISHORE: I know. Where is he today?

RUPA: The fifth. He will be at the ground.

KISHORE: The ground.

RUPA: The recruitment is today, sir. Two hundred candidates and their fathers, and every one of those fathers has been saving since October, and he has never once missed a recruitment day. It is where he found me.

SFX: A district ground. A loudspeaker with a dead cone. Two hundred pairs of rubber slippers on dry ground. A chair being dragged.

NARRATOR: The same ground. The same loudspeaker. The same month, one year on. Some things in this district never change, and on that particular morning that was the useful thing about it.

SFX: Under the crowd noise, close — a man's voice, warm, unhurried.

NAGESH: Sit, sit. How many marks in the written? Out of eighty? Beta, that is a very good number, and a very good number is exactly the difficulty, because a good number without a hand behind it is—

RUPA: Sahab.

SFX: Nothing.

NAGESH: Ah.

RUPA: Do not get up.

NAGESH: I was not going to. Beti — how many did you bring?

RUPA: All four registers. The receipt book from under the floor. And Pushpa, who is sitting in the yellow building signing a statement, and who is opening a window at Kachhwara next month with her name spelled correctly.

NAGESH: (a breath, almost a laugh) The floorboard. Nine years and it was the floorboard.

RUPA: It was not the floorboard. It was that you told her about Patna while you were eating.

SFX: The loudspeaker crackling. A whistle. The crowd noise changing — that ripple that goes through two hundred people when something is happening at the front.

KISHORE: (raised, on the loudspeaker, flat and official) The reading of the list is suspended for four minutes. Nobody leaves the ground. Nobody goes to the gate.

SFX: Two hundred people going quiet at once. Wind on a bad microphone.

KISHORE: There is a man on this ground who has, for three years, taken money from families for posts that do not exist. There is a book with the amounts and the dates and the thumb impressions, and it is in my hand, and it will be read out at this ground at eleven o'clock, entry by entry, so that every family in it hears their own name from me and not from a tea shop.

SFX: A sound from two hundred people that is not a cheer and not a shout.

KISHORE: Second. There has been an office above the bus stand at Barhagarh calling itself a Sahayak Thana. There is no such thing and there never was. Four hundred and nine matters were filed in it. Every one of those four hundred and nine is being taken into the register of this station from today and not one of them is being lost, and I will say the reason out loud because the reason is standing behind me.

SFX: Boots. A man being walked, unhurried, across dry ground, and two hundred people turning to watch it.

KISHORE: Third. The complaint against the boy from Kachhwara is withdrawn as of this morning and a case is registered against the labour contractor on twenty-two counts, on the strength of an advance book that a woman put in my hands at seven o'clock.

SARITA: (from the crowd, cracked, carrying) FOUR TIMES I WENT TO THE YELLOW BUILDING!

KISHORE: Four times. And it went into the pile on the left four times, and I put it there, and I have said that in a room above a bus stand and I am saying it on this ground.

SFX: The microphone handled. A pause. A crowd not moving.

KISHORE: The candidate list. Before I resume the reading, one correction to last year's roll, which I read out at this ground myself and which I am going to correct at this ground because that is where I read it.

SFX: A big sheet of paper. Held in wind.

KISHORE: Roll one-nine-six. Struck by pencil on the eleventh of the eighth month, on an initialled instruction that has been examined this morning and found to be nobody's. The strike is set aside. The candidate's marks stand — sixty-eight out of eighty in the written, three minutes twelve in the eight hundred.

SFX: Nothing. Long.

KISHORE: The candidate is on this ground. She brought me the papers herself at seven o'clock this morning, including the ones that are against her, and she is under enquiry for what she signed and she will answer every bit of it, and both of those things are true at the same time. Her name is Rupa.

SFX: Two hundred people. One voice starting it, at the back, and then the rest.

MUNNI: DIDI! DIDI, MY FORM — THEY TOOK MY FORM, IT IS ACCEPTED, ROLL FOUR-ELEVEN—

SFX: A girl running through a crowd. Somebody's tiffin box falling.

NARRATOR: The bench above the bus stand sat on the road for two more days because nobody had told them to stop coming. On the third day a table was put out in the compound of the yellow building, in the shade, with a chair on either side of it — one for the person who is asking and one for the person who writes it down and reads it back.

SFX: A stone room. High. A ceiling fan that works. A register — the heavy sound, but a different register, an older one, its spine cracking as it opens on a fresh page.

KISHORE: Sit. Not there. Behind the register. That is your side, until the enquiry says otherwise.

RUPA: Sir.

KISHORE: Land, Kachhwara. Call the first one.

SFX: A pen tested on the corner of a page. A nib that works. An ink pad. A rubber stamp lifted out of a tin.

RUPA: Land, Kachhwara. Come forward.

SFX: A chair opposite. A bundle in cloth set on the table.

RUPA: Number one. Say it from the beginning, and I will read it back to you.

SFX: Pen on paper. Fast, continuous. Then the stamp pressed into the pad, and onto the page — bottom of the first sheet, not only the last.

RUPA: B. A. R. H. A. G. A. R. H.
